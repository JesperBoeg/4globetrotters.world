Having a private driver in Sri Lanka — what to expect

August 2026 · Sri Lanka

Home › Blog › Having a private driver in Sri Lanka — what to expect

The usual way to get around Sri Lanka is with a private driver-guide, and that's what we did for our two weeks — the whole Sri Lanka trip, from Sigiriya down to the coast and back to the airport. On the whole it worked well and our guys were lovely. But it wasn't quite what we'd pictured, and a few things are worth knowing before you book.

One caveat up front: we're probably not the easiest clients in this regard. We've travelled more than most families do in a lifetime, and after years on the road we know pretty clearly what we want and what we don't — we'll spot a tourist trap or a bit of steering straight away, and we value a genuinely unique, off-the-beaten-track experience more than most. So it's quite possible we simply set our expectations too high, and that someone with less mileage and different needs would have been perfectly happy with exactly the same trip. Take the grumbles below in that spirit.

1. Expect them to be extremely helpful and friendly

This is the easy part. Ours were genuinely kind and easy to be around, and nothing was ever too much trouble. That side of it exceeded what we'd expected.

2. Don't expect a unique experience

Almost every stop is somewhere a normal tour bus would also pull in — the spice garden, the gem showroom, the standard viewpoints. It's a well-worn circuit and you're on it. That's fine if it's what you want, but don't expect to be taken anywhere secret.

3. Don't expect fluent English

Ours got by, but a fair amount got lost in the gaps. Don't assume a nuance has landed just because you said it — which makes the next point even more important.

4. Don't expect them to be on your side on price

This was the big one. The guide isn't a neutral advisor. On our first day, when it was time for lunch, ours drove straight to a classic tourist trap — buffet, tourist prices, the works. Left to him we'd have been steered to the big commercial gem showroom and the pricier shops too, presumably where there's some kind of relationship. None of it is hostile; it's just that his incentives and yours aren't the same, and it's worth remembering that every time a recommendation comes up.

Every operator promises a strict no-commission driver, and maybe that's literally true. But it's hard to see through: whether "commission" quietly excludes a free meal, a massage, a cut on what you spend in a particular shop, or some other favour, we couldn't tell. What we can say is that we were not at all convinced there was nothing commission-like going on. Assume the incentive is there in some form, and judge the recommendations accordingly.

5. Be much more explicit about your expectations than feels natural

For the first four or five days it was a gentle running negotiation until our guide worked out what kind of travellers we are — small local places rather than buffets, markets rather than showrooms. Combined with the language gap, the lesson is: say what you want plainly and early, and repeat it. It saves a lot of back-and-forth, and by the last two-thirds of the trip we were happily choosing all our own restaurants.

6. Don't let them book anything before you know where and how much

Ask for the name of the place and the price first, then check the reviews yourself before you agree. We caught overpriced options this way more than once — including a clinic in Arugam Bay (an IMC branch) that felt more geared to the bill than to Vitus's toe. A minute on your phone is usually all it takes.

7. You'll get a more unique experience arranging things yourself

The things we're happiest we did were the ones we set up or insisted on: the local surf lessons at Arugam Bay, eating where we chose, the little homestay by the rice paddies near Yala. If a memorable, slightly-off-the-track trip is what you're after, expect to build that part yourself.

8. If you want hassle-free and cost isn't the concern, it's brilliant

All that said — if what you want is a custom door-to-door trip with no logistics, no driving, and someone handling every transfer, ticket and hotel, a private driver is a genuinely great solution. For the famous, sell-out things especially — the Ella train tickets go in minutes — having an operator book ahead is exactly where the arrangement shines.

A few smaller things we learned

The driver you agree on isn't a guarantee. We'd booked a specific driver weeks ahead — the one listed with a minivan. The day before we arrived there was a "family emergency" and he was swapped for someone else, who happened to be listed with only a car/SUV. On arrival we were suddenly told this replacement also had a minivan, which seemed pretty unlikely. It may all have been perfectly innocent — but it happened a little too neatly for us to entirely believe it. Treat the named driver and vehicle as a hope, not a promise.

A couple more, minor but worth a mental note:

  • You're at the mercy of the itinerary's pace. Our Yala safari driver tried to head for the exit 45 minutes early; we had to insist on the full time we'd paid for.
  • They're people doing a job, and the car isn't quite your private space — ours borrowed Noah's phone to swap a SIM and message on one drive. Small thing, but worth expecting.

The bottom line

A private driver in Sri Lanka buys you comfort, zero hassle and lovely company. What it doesn't buy you is a guide whose interests are fully aligned with yours, or an off-the-beaten-path trip. Go in clear about what you want, check things yourself before you commit, and you'll get the best of both.
